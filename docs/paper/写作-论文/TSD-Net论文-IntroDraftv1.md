# TSD-Net: Tri-Source Decoupled Restoration for Low-Light Video Enhancement

---

## 1. Introduction

Low-light video enhancement is a long-standing yet challenging problem in computational photography, mobile imaging, and intelligent perception [1, 2]. Unlike its single-image counterpart, the video setting introduces a temporal dimension that serves as both a rich source of redundant information and a source of additional degradation. Neighboring frames provide complementary structure and context that can aid in recovering details buried in darkness; yet low-light videos simultaneously suffer from frame-to-frame brightness fluctuation, motion blur, sensor noise, rolling shutter artifacts, and temporal correspondence ambiguity [3, 4].

Single-image low-light enhancement methods, particularly Retinex-based models [5, 6, 7], have demonstrated strong capability in improving visibility through explicit illumination estimation and reflectance restoration. However, frame-wise application of these methods inevitably produces temporal inconsistency—flickering brightness, unstable noise patterns, and jittering color—since they inherently lack any mechanism to enforce coherence across time. On the other hand, general video restoration transformers, most notably [VRT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12288) [8], have shown the effectiveness of spatio-temporal attention for tasks such as video super-resolution, deblurring, and denoising. VRT employs temporal mutual self attention (TMSA), which partitions the video sequence into non-overlapping 2-frame clips and applies mutual attention for joint motion estimation, feature alignment, and fusion. To enable cross-clip interactions, the video sequence is shifted temporally for every other layer, reducing the computational complexity to $\mathcal{O}(T)$ while progressively enlarging the temporal receptive field. At the end of each network scale, parallel warping further fuses neighboring frame information into the current frame via optical flow estimation and deformable alignment. Despite its strong performance on uniform degradation tasks, VRT and its successors are not explicitly designed for the *illumination instability* that dominates low-light videos—they treat all temporal variations as motion to be aligned, conflating brightness fluctuation with spatial displacement. More recent video-specific approaches, including [SDSDNet](https://github.com/JIA-Lab-research/SDSD) [3] which introduces self-supervised denoising combined with Retinex-based illumination enhancement, [RetinexMCNet](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2025/papers/Wang_RetinexMCNet_A_Memory_Controller_Dominated_Network_for_Low-Light_Video_Enhancement_ICCV_2025_paper.pdf) [9] (ICCV 2025) which proposes a memory controller to integrate temporal keyframes for global consistency, and the [spatial-temporal consistent decomposition framework](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2025/238) [10] (IJCAI 2025) which leverages view-independent/view-dependent Retinex decomposition with cross-frame interaction, have advanced the field significantly. Nonetheless, **denoising**—the restoration of signal from noise—remains the central bottleneck that constrains all these methods.

A central difficulty in low-light enhancement is the **coupling of illumination degradation with imaging noise**. In dark scenes, sensor noise is spatially non-uniform—darker regions exhibit disproportionately higher noise variance due to the Poissonian-Gaussian nature of photon shot noise and read noise [11]. This coupling creates a dilemma: enhancing illumination amplifies noise, while suppressing noise prior to illumination adjustment risks destroying fine details that are already dim. The handling of noise within the overall model architecture can be broadly categorized into three design paradigms.

**(i) Sequential design** follows a "first brighten, then denoise" pipeline. [RetinexNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04560) [6] (BMVC 2018) decomposes the image into reflectance and illumination, adjusts the illumination, and applies separate denoising on the reflectance branch. [Retinexformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06705) [5] (ICCV 2023) refines this into a one-stage Retinex-based framework (ORF), where an illumination estimator first produces a light-up map $\bar{L}$, and a corruption restorer—the Illumination-Guided Transformer (IGT)—subsequently handles the amplified noise, artifacts, under-/over-exposure, and color distortion introduced by the light-up process. For video, [SDSDNet](https://doi.org/10.1109/iccv48922.2021.00956) [3] follows a similar strategy with self-supervised denoising after Retinex-based illumination enhancement, and [RetinexMCNet](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2025/papers/Wang_RetinexMCNet_A_Memory_Controller_Dominated_Network_for_Low-Light_Video_Enhancement_ICCV_2025_paper.pdf) [9] designs a channel-aware Illumination Adjustment Module (IAM) followed by an illumination-guided Reflectance Denoising Module (RDM). While conceptually clean, the sequential paradigm suffers from **error propagation**: residual noise in the pre-enhancement stage is amplified by the light-up step, and there is no feedback mechanism to correct illumination estimation based on noise characteristics.

**(ii) Implicit design** embeds denoising within the model's internal representations rather than treating it as a separate stage. Transformer-based U-shaped architectures [8, 12] rely on the global relational modeling capacity of self-attention to implicitly separate signal from noise during restoration. [VRT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12288) [8] achieves this through spatio-temporal mutual attention that jointly extracts, aligns, and fuses features across frames, implicitly performing denoising as a byproduct of multi-frame aggregation. [DWTA-Net](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2510.09450) [13] further advances the implicit paradigm with a Mamba-based architecture where Stage I stabilizes brightness and coarse structure, while Stage II performs recurrent temporal aggregation as a natural denoiser. The advantage is end-to-end simplicity; the limitation is **interpretability and controllability**—the model cannot explicitly modulate its denoising behavior based on local illumination or motion conditions, leading to over-smoothing in well-lit regions or insufficient noise suppression in dark areas.

**(iii) Degradation decoupling design** explicitly decomposes the degradation into distinct sources and processes each with specialized sub-networks. This divide-and-conquer philosophy promises better interpretability, task-specific optimization, and avoidance of gradient conflicts between competing objectives. [DualDn](https://www.ecva.net/papers/eccv_2024/papers_ECCV/papers/07547.pdf) [14] (ECCV 2024) separates denoising into raw-domain and sRGB-domain branches connected by a differentiable ISP; [PDHAT](https://doi.org/10.1109/tmm.2024.3355634) [15] (TMM 2024) decouples color/brightness perception from edge/detail perception via two heterogeneous auxiliary tasks; [RDDM](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/html/Liu_Residual_Denoising_Diffusion_Models_CVPR_2024_paper.html) [16] (CVPR 2024) decouples the diffusion process into residual diffusion (certainty) and noise diffusion (diversity). It is to this paradigm that our work belongs—yet with a critical extension from two sources to three.

A natural question arises: *why three sources, and what is the principled basis for this particular decomposition?* Most existing decoupling approaches settle on **two-source** divisions, which can be further grouped as follows.

**By physical origin in the imaging pipeline.** [DualDn](https://openimaginglab.github.io/DualDn/) [14] decomposes denoising into raw-domain and sRGB-domain branches: the raw denoiser handles sensor-specific, spatially varying noise with calibrated noise maps, while the sRGB denoiser addresses ISP-amplified and correlated residual noise. [PDHAT](https://doi.org/10.1109/tmm.2024.3355634) [15], targeting joint low-light enhancement and deblurring, constructs two heterogeneous auxiliary tasks—coarse preview prediction (CPP) for color/brightness perception and high-frequency reconstruction (HFR) for edge/detail perception—to decouple perceptual dimensions for parallel processing. While effective in their respective domains, these two-source divisions are **task-specific rather than degradation-statistics-driven**: they split based on *where* in the pipeline or *what* perceptual attribute to process, not based on the intrinsic temporal behavior of the degradation itself.

**By mathematical/statistical properties.** [RDDM](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Liu_Residual_Denoising_Diffusion_Models_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf) [16] decouples the diffusion process into residual diffusion (directional certainty from target to input) and noise diffusion (random perturbation for diversity), employing two independent coefficient schedules. This mathematical decomposition is elegant and general, but it lacks **physical grounding specific to low-light video**—the residual vs. noise dichotomy does not distinguish between illumination-induced and motion-induced deviations, which behave very differently in the temporal domain.

**By frequency/transform domain.** [CRWKV](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2025/86) [17] (IJCAI 2025) employs a Frequency Mix (FMix) module to isolate noise in high-frequency spectra and integrates it with spatial representations through multi-view learning. [CoDe](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/html/Gu_CoDe_An_Explicit_Content_Decoupling_Framework_for_Image_Restoration_CVPR_2024_paper.html) [18] (CVPR 2024) decouples content components according to frequency spectra adaptively generated from the transform domain in a divide-and-conquer architecture. [FADN](https://doi.org/10.1109/tnnls.2024.3439591) [19] (TNNLS 2024) uses wavelet transform to separate low- and high-frequency representations for progressive denoising with residual compensation. While effective, frequency-domain decomposition is **agnostic to the temporal behavior** of the degradation—it cannot distinguish between high-frequency noise (temporally random) and high-frequency motion edges (temporally structured), which require fundamentally different processing strategies.

Despite these advances, existing two-source strategies share a common limitation when applied to low-light video: **they collapse two fundamentally different temporal processes into a single residual category.** In Retinex-based methods, the reflectance component $R$ absorbs both sensor noise and motion blur, yet noise is temporally independent and benefits from aggressive temporal averaging, while motion introduces structured temporal displacement that must be compensated rather than averaged. In DualDn, the sRGB-domain residual combines both ISP-amplified noise and any motion-induced artifacts. In RDDM, the "residual" captures all systematic deviation from the target—both illumination drift and motion displacement—without distinction. This conflation creates three fundamental challenges. **First**, illumination variation, imaging noise, and motion perturbation exhibit distinct temporal dynamics: illumination changes slowly across frames (bias-type corruption), noise is temporally uncorrelated (variance-type corruption), and motion introduces structured correspondence breakdown (displacement-type corruption). Applying the same temporal processing strategy to all three is suboptimal. **Second**, the three sources interact in a cascade: noise is amplified by illumination adjustment, motion breaks the temporal correspondence needed for multi-frame denoising, and illumination estimation is misled by both noise and motion. A monolithic or two-source approach cannot model these interactions explicitly. **Third**, the optimal processing order and strategy differs per source: illumination correction benefits from spatial smoothness priors, denoising benefits from temporal averaging under reliable correspondence, and motion compensation requires explicit alignment or compensation—but these branches must still interact, as denoising strength should be modulated by motion confidence and illumination level.

Driven by the above analysis, we argue that low-light video enhancement should be reformulated as a **tri-source decoupled restoration (TSDR)** problem. The core insight is that illumination degradation, imaging noise, and motion perturbation—the three dominant degradation sources in low-light video—should be **decomposed early, processed through dedicated specialized branches, and re-integrated in a stage-wise manner**. We propose **TSD-Net** (Tri-Source Decoupled Network), an end-to-end framework built on the TSDR principle. At the heart of TSD-Net is a tri-source decoupling strategy that operates at three levels: **(1)** at the frequency level, a Spatial Wavelet Diverter (SWD) performs Haar DWT-based subband routing, directing low-frequency (LL) content to illumination processing and high-frequency (HF) content to noise/motion branches; **(2)** at the temporal correspondence level, a Temporal Correspondence & Alignment (TCA) module computes an illumination-invariant frame-to-frame attention matrix $C_{t,\Omega}$, whose diagonal structure encodes motion reliability and serves as a source prior to gate the noise vs. motion processing; **(3)** at the source prior level, a Temporal-Frequency Degradation Estimator (TFDE) maps the time-frequency features to two scalar field priors $s_{\text{illu}}$ and $s_{\text{noise}}$, representing local illumination deficiency and noise intensity respectively. These priors, together with $C_{t,\Omega}$, guide three parallel downstream modules—an Illumination-Source Processing Network (ISPN) for illumination correction, a Noise Degradation Processing Network (NDPN) for $C_{t,\Omega}$-guided adaptive denoising, and a Motion Compensation Processing Network (MCPN) for motion artifact removal—whose outputs are fused through a Stage-wise Guided Restoration & Fusion (SGRF) head with perceptual decoupling.

Specifically, ISPN generates a spatially-adaptive illumination attention map $A_{\text{illu}} \in [0,1]^{H \times W}$ and a pixel-wise brightening map, where $A_{\text{illu}} \to 1$ in dark regions for strong illumination boost and $A_{\text{illu}} \to 0$ in well-exposed regions for minimal adjustment. A temporal anchoring mechanism with a zero-initialized learnable gate suppresses frame-to-frame illumination flicker. NDPN utilizes $C_{t,\Omega}$ as a temporal confidence map: high diagonal values enable strong temporal denoising via aligned neighbor frame aggregation to preserve spatial detail; low values force the network to rely on spatial-only denoising within the center frame, thereby avoiding motion-induced ghosting. MCPN targets motion-affected regions identified through low $C_{t,\Omega}$ values, applying dedicated compensation to restore blurred or misaligned structures. To avoid gradient conflicts between competing restoration objectives during training, we design a two-phase schedule: Phase 1 isolates the ISPN pathway by zeroing NDPN/MCPN outputs, allowing illumination learning to converge without interference; Phase 2 gradually unlocks all branches with a cross-excitation gate and a perceptual decoupling strategy that assigns SSIM loss to the denoising output (S1), VGG perceptual loss to the deblurring output (S2), and Charbonnier loss to the illumination-enhanced output (S3).

Our contributions are three-fold:
1. We reformulate low-light video enhancement as a **tri-source decoupled restoration (TSDR)** problem, providing a principled decomposition into illumination, noise, and motion sources based on their distinct temporal statistics—extending beyond the prevailing two-source paradigm with explicit physical and statistical justification.
2. We design **TSD-Net** with wavelet-based subband routing, illumination-invariant temporal correspondence priors, and three dedicated source-specific processing branches (ISPN, NDPN, MCPN), enabling source-aware adaptive restoration with explicit inter-branch interaction through $C_{t,\Omega}$-guided gating.
3. We introduce a **stage-wise training schedule with perceptual decoupling** to resolve gradient conflicts between competing restoration objectives, coupled with Kendall uncertainty weighting for automatic loss balancing.

## 3. Proposed Method

### 3.1 Tri-Source Decoupled Restoration (TSDR) Framework

We formulate low-light video enhancement as a **tri-source decoupled restoration** problem. Let $\{I_{t-k}, \dots, I_t, \dots, I_{t+k}\}$ denote a sliding window of $T = 2k+1$ consecutive low-light frames centered at the target frame $I_t$. Following the low-light imaging model [11, 20], each observed frame $I_t$ is modeled as a composite of three underlying degradation sources acting upon a latent clean signal $X_t$:

$$I_t = \mathcal{D}\big(\underbrace{\mathcal{I}(X_t)}_{\text{illumination}},\; \underbrace{\mathcal{N}_t}_{\text{noise}},\; \underbrace{\mathcal{M}_t}_{\text{motion}}\big),$$

where $\mathcal{I}(\cdot)$ represents the illumination degradation operator that suppresses exposure and disrupts brightness constancy, $\mathcal{N}_t$ denotes the sensor noise term modeled as heteroscedastic Poissonian-Gaussian noise with variance dependent on local illumination level, and $\mathcal{M}_t$ accounts for motion-induced corruption including blur, misalignment, and rolling shutter artifacts. The composite operator $\mathcal{D}$ captures the entangled interaction of these three sources in the observed image.

More concretely, adopting the single-frame noise model of Foi *et al.* [11] and Wang *et al.* [3], the observed pixel intensity at spatial location $p$ satisfies:

$$I_t(p) = L_t(p) \cdot X_t(p) + n_t(p), \quad n_t(p) \sim \mathcal{N}\big(0,\; \sigma_s^2 \cdot L_t(p) \cdot X_t(p) + \sigma_r^2\big),$$

where $L_t(p) \in (0, 1]$ is the illumination attenuation at pixel $p$, and $\sigma_s^2$, $\sigma_r^2$ are the shot noise and read noise parameters respectively. Extending this to the multi-frame setting, the temporal dimension introduces motion displacement $\mathbf{d}_{t \to t'}(p)$ between frames $t$ and $t'$:

$$I_{t'}(p) \approx L_{t'}(p) \cdot X_t\big(p + \mathbf{d}_{t \to t'}(p)\big) + n_{t'}(p).$$

The key observation motivating our tri-source formulation is that **the three degradation sources exhibit fundamentally different temporal statistics**, as summarized in Table 1:

| Property | Illumination $\mathcal{I}$ | Noise $\mathcal{N}$ | Motion $\mathcal{M}$ |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Temporal type** | Bias (slowly varying) | Variance (i.i.d. across $t$) | Displacement (structured) |
| **Frame correlation** | $\|L_t - L_{t-1}\| \to 0$ | $\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_t \cdot \mathcal{N}_{t'}] \approx 0,\; t \neq t'$ | $\mathbf{d}_{t \to t'} \neq 0$ in moving regions |
| **Optimal strategy** | Spatial smoothness prior | Temporal averaging under correspondence | Alignment / compensation |
| **Amplification chain** | Amplifies noise via $\nabla\mathcal{I}$ | Degrades $\mathcal{I}$ estimation | Breaks correspondence for denoising |

This taxonomy reveals why **a two-source split is insufficient**: any grouping that merges two of the three sources into a single branch conflates degradation types with incompatible temporal statistics.

- **Illumination + Noise** vs. Motion (Retinex-style): The "reflectance" branch $R = I / L$ absorbs both noise and clean texture. Noise amplification during the $I/L$ division makes subsequent denoising harder, as the noise variance becomes spatially dependent on the potentially inaccurate $L$ estimate.
- **Illumination + Motion** vs. Noise: The "structural" branch absorbs both slowly varying illumination and structured motion, preventing the model from exploiting the temporal smoothness of $L$ for flicker suppression.
- **Noise + Motion** vs. Illumination: The "residual" branch absorbs both random noise and structured motion. Since noise benefits from temporal averaging while motion requires compensation, the unified branch must simultaneously denoise *and* deblur—objectives whose optimal temporal processing strategies are diametrically opposed.

In contrast, our tri-source decomposition assigns each source to a dedicated branch with **matched temporal processing**: ISPN exploits cross-frame illumination smoothness for flicker-free light-up; NDPN leverages temporal averaging under $C_{t,\Omega}$-verified correspondence for noise suppression; MCPN applies motion-specific compensation where correspondence fails. The three branches interact through the shared $C_{t,\Omega}$ matrix and source priors $s_{\text{illu}}$, $s_{\text{noise}}$, ensuring that the decoupling does not sacrifice information flow.

The **sequential coupling** of these sources further demands explicit decoupling. Noise is amplified by illumination enhancement ($\nabla\mathcal{I}$ amplifies $\mathcal{N}_t$); motion breaks the temporal correspondence needed for multi-frame denoising ($\mathcal{M}_t$ degrades $C_{t,\Omega}$); and illumination estimation is misled by both noise and motion artifacts. This coupling motivates our design choice to perform decoupling *early* in the feature space—via wavelet-domain frequency分流 and illumination-invariant temporal correspondence—before routing to source-specific branches.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, TSD-Net first extracts multi-frame shallow features $\{F_{t'}\}_{t'=0}^{T-1}$ through a shared encoder, then performs three parallel decomposition operations:

**(1) Frequency-domain routing via SWD.** For each frame feature $F_{t'} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$, the Spatial Wavelet Diverter (SWD) applies a Haar Discrete Wavelet Transform to decompose it into four subbands: LL, LH, HL, and HH. A learnable routing coefficient $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ (output by a lightweight $\alpha$-net) controls the subband-level allocation. The LL subband, containing low-frequency illumination and structural information, is primarily routed to the TFDE for illumination prior estimation. The three high-frequency subbands, which encode texture and edge information less affected by global illumination variation, are routed to the TCA for temporal correspondence learning. A weak regularization loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{swd}} = (\bar{\alpha} - 0.5)^2$ prevents extreme routing imbalance.

**(2) Temporal correspondence estimation via TCA.** The TCA module processes the wavelet HF features using a 4-directional spatial WKV scanning mechanism to compute an illumination-invariant attention matrix $C_{t,\Omega}$, where $\Omega = \{t-k, \dots, t-1, t+1, \dots, t+k\}$ denotes the set of neighboring frames. The diagonal values $C_{t,\Omega}[i, i]$ serve as a **motion reliability indicator**: values near 1 indicate static, well-corresponded pixels ideal for temporal denoising; values near 0 indicate motion, occlusion, or photometric inconsistency requiring motion compensation or spatial-only processing. The TCA simultaneously produces an aligned center-frame feature $\hat{F}_t$ through correspondence-weighted aggregation.

**(3) Degradation prior estimation via TFDE.** The Temporal-Frequency Degradation Estimator takes the wavelet LL features and outputs two scalar field priors: $s_{\text{illu}} \in [0, 1]^{H \times W}$, encoding local illumination deficiency (higher values indicate darker regions requiring more boost), and $s_{\text{noise}} \in [0, 1]^{H \times W}$, encoding estimated noise intensity (higher values indicate stronger noise requiring more aggressive denoising). These priors, together with $C_{t,\Omega}$, constitute the **tri-source prior set** $\mathcal{P} = \{s_{\text{illu}}, s_{\text{noise}}, C_{t,\Omega}\}$ that gates the downstream processing.

The tri-source prior set $\mathcal{P}$ is then dispatched to three dedicated processing branches: $s_{\text{illu}}$ and $\hat{F}_t$ drive **ISPN** for illumination correction; $s_{\text{noise}}$ and the diagonal of $C_{t,\Omega}$ guide **NDPN** for confidence-adaptive denoising; and the off-diagonal motion structure of $C_{t,\Omega}$ drives **MCPN** for motion compensation. The outputs of these three branches are fused through a **Stage-wise Guided Restoration & Fusion (SGRF)** pipeline that sequentially applies denoising (S1), deblurring (S2), and illumination enhancement (S3), with each stage receiving matched loss supervision under our perceptual decoupling strategy. This tri-level decomposition—frequency routing, temporal correspondence, and degradation priors—ensures that the three sources are separated early in the feature space, processed with source-appropriate temporal strategies, and re-integrated with explicit cross-branch interaction.
